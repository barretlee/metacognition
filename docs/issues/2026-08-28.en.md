## 1. 🧠 Cognitive Science — What You Smell Is Never Scent, It's Memory

You've had this experience: some unguarded moment, a wisp of scent drifts in — a certain brand of detergent, rain-soaked earth, old book pages, the smell of frying in a grandmother's kitchen — and then, suddenly, you are hurled back a decade. Not "remembering" a decade ago, but *being* there, the temperature and the light and your own heartbeat all restored at once. This isn't sentimentality. It's your sense of smell doing something no other sense can do.

Neuroscience holds a counterintuitive fact: of all the human senses, **smell is the only one that does not pass through the thalamus.** Vision, hearing, touch, taste — every signal must first stop at the thalamus, the "switchboard" where it is queued, classified, and relayed. Smell alone takes a bypass: the signal from the olfactory receptors in your nose shoots straight into the amygdala and the hippocampus — the former governing emotion, the latter memory. Every other sense is "analyze first, feel later." Smell is **feel first, analyze later** — and often it skips the analysis entirely. That's why a scent can detonate an emotion and a memory before you even have time to ask why.

Stranger still: the sense we use every day and ignore every day is the one science cracked the latest. In 1991, Linda Buck and Richard Axel published a paper in *Cell* identifying the gene family behind olfactory receptors — in mammals, the **largest gene family in the genome**, with mice carrying about a thousand and humans left with roughly four hundred still-functional copies. The discovery earned them the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Think about it: humans walked on the moon in 1969, but didn't learn *how we smell* until 1991. The oldest sense was the last one decoded.

And for anyone who works with multimodal models, smell holds a particularly pointed identity: **it is the one modality AI remains completely blind to.** Your models can "read" text, "see" images, "hear" audio, but they know nothing of scent — yet scent is the input closest to emotion and memory, the one most likely to drive a human to act. Smell's absence means that every intelligence claiming to be "general" today is missing the most primitive emotional highway a human has. That's not a technical detail; it's a reminder of how far our machines still are from understanding a person.

As a footnote, olfaction is also one of the few neural systems that keeps regenerating throughout adult life — the neurons of the olfactory epithelium renew for as long as you live. And in reverse, a sudden decline in smell is emerging as one of the earliest warning signs of Parkinson's and Alzheimer's (a pandemic's worth of people discovered "I can't smell anything" first-hand these past few years). The oldest sense holds two keys at once — "deepest memory" and "earliest disease alarm" — and you have probably never once, in your whole life, stopped to actually smell something.

- 📖 Diane Ackerman, *A Natural History of the Senses* — the chapter on smell is especially beautiful
- 📄 Buck & Axel (1991), *Cell*, "A novel multigene family may encode odorant receptors" — the Nobel-winning original paper
- 📖 Marcel Proust, *In Search of Lost Time*, Vol. 1 "Swann's Way" — the madeleine passage is the very source of the term "Proust effect"
- 🎯 Action: tonight, find a scent that can "teleport" you, inhale deeply, and write down where it took you. You just ran the brain's oldest highway by hand

## 2. 🏛️ Philosophy — Epicurus Dismantled Your Fear of Death Two Thousand Years Ago

If Stoicism teaches you how to *endure*, the school next door — the one that has been catastrophically misunderstood — teaches something else: **most of the things you fear don't actually exist.** The man was Epicurus, born 341 BCE, who opened a school in a garden in Athens — a school that, extraordinarily for the third century BCE, **admitted women and slaves.** In its own time, that was almost a provocation.

The word "Epicurean" has, for two millennia, been a synonym for hedonism, for eating, drinking, and self-indulgence. It is a two-thousand-year-old case of defamation. Epicurus did say "pleasure is the good," but his definition of pleasure is the opposite of what we imagine: pleasure is not indulgence, it is the **absence of pain** — freedom from bodily distress (*aponia*) and freedom from mental anxiety (*ataraxia*). His own life was almost ascetic: bread, water, and a small pot of cheese as a feast. What he chased was **calm**, not stimulation. A man who equated happiness with "not being agitated" was posthumously twisted into the patron saint of gluttony — arguably the most successful smear job in the history of philosophy.

His most famous strike was aimed at humanity's deepest fear — **the fear of death.** He said, in a line quoted ever since: **death is nothing to us; for when we exist, death is not, and when death is, we do not exist.** This is the famous "symmetry argument": you are not the least bit afraid of the infinitely long stretch of time before you were born, during which you did not exist — so why fear the equally infinite stretch after your death, during which you will likewise not exist? To you, the two are the same "nothing."

Epicurus packaged all this into a "fourfold remedy" (the *tetrapharmakos*), four sentences: **Don't fear god, don't worry about death, what is good is easy to get, what is terrible is easy to endure.** Translated into plain terms — don't fear divine punishment (his atomism reduced the world to atoms and void, with no god keeping a ledger); don't fear death (once you're dead you know nothing, so you won't "know" you're afraid); happiness is easy to obtain (bread and water suffice); and pain is easy to bear (sharp pain is short, long pain is mild). Four sentences, aimed at uprooting the four great sources of human anxiety in one stroke.

For a modern person — especially one driven by legacy, influence, and the fear of being forgotten — this is a dose of sobriety. All your anxiety about "will my work survive me," "will I be remembered," rests, in Epicurus's view, on a single illusion: **you imagine you'll be there to witness it.** But you won't. This is not nihilism — it is, for him, the most complete liberation on offer: you don't need to be anxious about a future you won't inhabit, which lets you pull your entire attention back into this life, the one you're actually living right now. The Stoics told you to let go of fate; Epicurus tells you to let go of death.

As a footnote, this nearly-lost philosophy was rescued by a poem. The Roman poet Lucretius wrote *De Rerum Natura* ("On the Nature of Things"), setting Epicurean thought to verse. In 1417, a scribe named Poggio Bracciolini dug a forgotten manuscript of it out of a monastery, and the rest — as Pulitzer-winner Stephen Greenblatt told in *The Swerve* — is the story of "how an old book helped ignite the Renaissance." Ideas, it turns out, can lie buried in the earth for over a thousand years and then suddenly germinate.

- 📖 Epicurus, "Letter to Menoeceus" and "Principal Doctrines" — the originals are extremely short; two hours and the impact beats any paraphrase
- 📖 Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* — atomism and "death is nothing to fear," turned into poetry
- 📖 Stephen Greenblatt, *The Swerve* — how one old book changed the world; Pulitzer Prize
- 🎯 Action: write down your most recent bout of anxiety about death / being forgotten / leaving nothing behind, then ask the symmetry question: will the you of that moment actually be there?

## 3. 💰 Investing & Personal Finance — That Extra Dollar You Earn Is Worth Far More After Tax Than You Think

There is a myth that is widely and sincerely believed: **"Don't take the raise, the promotion, the extra client — once your income crosses into a higher bracket, you'll actually take home less."** If you've ever half-believed this, congratulations: you've been had by the word "marginal."

The individual income tax in the overwhelming majority of countries is **progressive** — the higher your income, the higher the rate. But progressive does not mean "all or nothing": income is sliced into bands, and each band is taxed only on the portion that falls within it. Crossing into a higher bracket means **only the amount above the threshold** is taxed at the higher rate; everything below it keeps its original rate. So every extra dollar you earn is worth more after tax than before — only the *marginal* dollar pays a higher share. This is the difference between the **marginal tax rate** ("how much tax does my next dollar pay") and the **effective tax rate** ("what did I pay on average across all my income"). The effective rate is always lower, because the lower bands drag the average down.

The real sting of this isn't tax — it's the thinking tool hiding underneath that most engineers lack: **thinking at the margin.** Engineers ask "how much in total?" and "what's the average?" Economists ask "what does the *next* unit do?" And those two questions give opposite answers. For an airline with an empty seat on a flight that's leaving anyway, the marginal cost of one more passenger is near zero, so the seat can be sold for scraps; for you writing code, the marginal value of "one more feature" can be negative — even though you already spent a whole day on it. But that day is a **sunk cost**, and it should not influence the question "should I keep going for the next hour?" Marginal thinking forces you to ask only one thing: **looking forward from this exact moment, is one more step worth it?**

Move this frame onto your own decisions: whether to take another project depends not on "how much does it pay in total" but on "is it worth more than whatever my next twenty hours would otherwise do"; whether to scroll for ten more minutes depends not on "it's only ten minutes" but on "the marginal output of these ten minutes is already near zero, and your next ten minutes are being stolen." The moment you start accounting in "next units" instead of "averages," a pile of murky dilemmas suddenly snaps into focus.

The deeper lesson of tax is this: **a progressive system is, in effect, punishing you at the margin — and most people, because they don't understand the margin, imagine the punishment to be bigger than it is, and so voluntarily surrender increments they could have claimed.** Don't let a math error make your life decisions for you. Go claim what's yours.

- 📖 N. Gregory Mankiw, *Principles of Economics* — the chapters on "thinking at the margin" are the single most stealable tool in economics
- 📖 Thomas Sowell, *Basic Economics* — makes plain that "the tax burden is not what you think it is"
- 🎧 Search "marginal vs effective tax rate" — any reputable explainer; spend ten minutes truly grasping how your jurisdiction's brackets are sliced
- 🎯 Action: look up the progressive bracket table where you live, and by hand compute "if I earned one more unit, how much would I actually keep" — then burn that number into your memory

## 4. 🎨 Literature — Hemingway's Iceberg Theory Is About Omission, Not Rhetoric

In *Death in the Afternoon* (1932), Hemingway wrote a passage that became one of the most-quoted in twentieth-century writing: "If a writer of prose knows enough of what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. **The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water.**"

That is the iceberg theory — also called the theory of omission. On the surface it's about craft; underneath, it's a philosophy of **restraint**. Hemingway's sentences are short, hard, nearly adjective-free, telegraphic. Not because he could only write short, but because he believed: **the things you truly know will seep out through the parts you leave out.** The famous six-word story attributed to him — "For sale: baby shoes, never worn" — hits precisely because it says nothing: no grief, no explanation, not a single adjective, and yet you have already completed an entire tragedy in your head.

But the most counterintuitive — and most misunderstood — part of the iceberg theory is this: **the precondition for omission is that you genuinely know the seven-eighths.** Hemingway's point is that a writer must know enough about what lies beneath the surface to be *entitled* to omit it. If what you're omitting is something you never understood, that's not an iceberg — that's a hole. The eighth above water has power only because the seven-eighths beneath it is **solid** — truly lived, truly understood, truly mastered. In other words: **depth is not how much you say; it's how much you have that you can afford not to say.**

What does this have to do with a creator, a person who lives inside the information stream? Everything. We live in an age of over-expression — everyone talking, everyone writing, everyone using AI to generate a thousand words a second, filling every inch of blank space. And precisely in such an age, **restraint becomes a scarce skill.** You don't have to pour out everything you know; you don't even have to say everything you mean. Negative space, omission, saying less — that's what makes content float. And one layer deeper: you cannot fake depth through omission — if you haven't truly mastered a thing, what remains after you omit is only emptiness. So the iceberg theory ends up forcing you to do the dumbest, most valuable work there is: **grow the seven-eighths beneath the water first.**

- 📖 Hemingway, *Death in the Afternoon* — the original source of the iceberg theory; that passage is worth reading three times
- 📖 Hemingway, "The Snows of Kilimanjaro" and "Hills Like White Elephants" — watch omission work inside real stories
- 📖 Hemingway, *The Complete Short Stories* — the six-word story, and a shelf of textbook-grade "less is more"
- 🎯 Action: take a passage you wrote recently (a post, an email, some copy), delete half the words, keep only the load-bearing ones, and reread it — see if it didn't just get stronger

## 5. ⚖️ Law — A Patent Is a "Monopoly for Disclosure" Bargain, and AI Is Now Stuck at Its Door

The patent system is, at bottom, a deal — and a cunningly designed one. The state says: I grant you a **time-limited monopoly** — typically twenty years from the filing date, during which no one may use your invention without permission — but in exchange, you must **fully disclose** it, publicly, to the point where "a person skilled in the art" could reproduce it from your filing alone. After twenty years, the invention falls into the public domain, free for anyone to use, and everyone gets to stand on your shoulders and build the next thing. That's the "disclosure for monopoly" bargain: a short-term monopoly, purchased at the price of permanent disclosure.

The logic reaches back to the Venetian Patent Statute of 1474 and England's Statute of Monopolies of 1624 — one of the few institutions human beings invented that genuinely pushed civilization forward. It resolves an ancient dilemma: an inventor must sink enormous time and money into R&D, but the moment the thing exists, others can copy it for almost nothing. Without patents, who would invest? But if inventions stayed secret forever, how would technology spread? The genius of the patent is that it takes **both sides** — rewarding innovation with monopoly first, then feeding the next round of innovation through expiry.

But this elegant system is now jammed on an unprecedented question: **what if the invention wasn't made by a person, but by an AI?** In recent years, a man named Stephen Thaler filed patent applications in the US, Europe, the UK, and elsewhere, listing his AI system "DABUS" as the inventor. Nearly all were rejected. The UK Supreme Court settled it at the end of 2023: **an inventor must be a "natural person."** A machine can invent, but the law only recognizes a human. Which opens an absurd vacuum: an AI-generated invention that fully satisfies all three requirements — novelty, inventive step, utility — can find **no legal inventor at all.** You can't name the machine, and does the person who merely pressed Enter count as having made a "creative contribution"?

For an AI practitioner, this is not a distant piece of legal trivia — it is a crack opening directly beneath your feet. Your models, your agents, your automation pipelines are "participating" in creation at an ever-higher rate, from generating code to proposing molecular structures. And current patent law has no answer for where the boundary between human and machine lies — which means **who "owns" AI-generated inventions is, right now, a legal gray zone.** Some argue it belongs to the company that trained the model, some to the human who "used" the model, some that the AI should be the inventor itself. Until this is settled, the most valuable "creations" of the AI age have no clear property rights — and without clear property rights, capital hesitates and innovation stalls. You are cheerfully building intelligence at the frontier, while the law at the rear still clings to the two-hundred-year-old assumption that only a natural person can be an inventor.

- 📖 William Rosen, *The Most Powerful Idea in the World* — how the patent system drove the Industrial Revolution; a great read
- 📄 UK Supreme Court, 2023, *Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks* — the authoritative "an inventor must be a natural person" ruling
- 🎧 Search "DABUS AI inventor" — watch how nearly every jurisdiction has, almost in unison, refused to let a machine be an inventor
- 🎯 Action: think of something you recently "came up with" with the help of AI, and ask in earnest: if it were patentable, who would the law write in the "inventor" box?
