## 1. 🧠 Cognitive Science — You Actually Think With Your Body

In 1980, linguist George Lakoff and philosopher Mark Johnson published a slim book called *Metaphors We Live By*, and with one sentence they dismantled two thousand years of illusions about "reason."

Their point is disarmingly simple: metaphor is not a poet's rhetorical trick — it is the *only* way abstract thought works. You cannot "think" about time, argument, or morality directly, because they are invisible and intangible. Instead, you *map* them onto concrete, bodily experiences. So time becomes money — you "spend" time, "save" time, "waste" time, "invest" time. Argument becomes war — you "attack" a position, "defend" a view, "shoot down" a claim. Good and bad become up and down — spirits are "high," moods "sink," you "fall into" depression. These are not figures of speech; they are the actual cognitive structure of your mind. A species with no body, that never felt hunger or fought or stood upright against gravity, could never use these coordinates to "understand" the world.

Lakoff's follow-through is the sharpest part: even the purest, most mathematical-looking concepts — causation, quantity, category — trace back to bodily experience. "Causation" comes from the feeling of pushing an object; "more is up" comes from stacking things higher. What we call "pure reason" is just body-metaphor piled on body-metaphor until you can no longer see the foundation.

This stings most for anyone building AI. The trillions of tokens you feed a large model are saturated with these bodily metaphors — "attacking an argument," "the cost of time," "the moral high ground." The "reasoning" a model learns is, at bottom, symbol-shuffling inside a metaphorical system that only a body can truly ground. That points to an uncomfortable question: can a system that has never been hungry, never felt pain, never pushed anything, never stood in gravity ever truly "understand" the abstract concepts it speaks about — or is it merely fluently reciting a bodily grammar it can never firsthand verify? The embodied-cognition school will tell you, coldly: intelligence may not live in abstract symbols at all, but in the interaction between a body and a world. The "pure text leads to AGI" road you're betting on had a landmine planted under it forty years ago.

## 2. 💰 Investing — Bonds Contain a Hidden Leverage Called Duration

Technologists usually think of bonds as "safe assets" — you lend money, collect interest, get your principal back. Far safer than stocks. That intuition shatters on a single number called **duration**.

Duration's first meaning is simple: how long, on average, you must wait to receive a bond's cash flows. But its second meaning is the lethal one — **modified duration measures price sensitivity to interest rates**; in plain terms, it is the built-in "leverage" of a bond. Roughly: a duration of 10 means a 1% move in rates moves the bond's price about 10% in the opposite direction. A 30-year zero-coupon bond has a duration near 30 — a 1-point rate rise can knock nearly 30% off its price. That is not a "safe asset"; that is an interest-rate future wearing a bond costume.

The world learned this lesson in real money in 2022. To crush inflation, the Fed yanked rates up from near zero, and long-dated Treasuries fell roughly 30% that year — the worst year for "the safest asset" in many people's memory. The most extreme case was Silicon Valley Bank, which collapsed in March 2023: it took in deposits and parked them in long-duration bonds bought in the low-rate era. When rates rose, those bonds' market value cratered, producing huge unrealized losses; when depositors ran, SVB had to sell at a loss, the losses became real, and the bank died. **It wasn't bad loans or fraud that killed it — it was duration risk**: an asset that "looked perfectly safe" paired with a leverage number nobody was watching.

For people who don't trade bonds, this matters three ways. First, the guts of your pension, your money-market funds, and your bond ETFs are all exposed to duration — you think you're "saving," but you're implicitly holding rate leverage. Second, a homeowner is effectively "short duration": your mortgage rate is the duration cost of the money you borrowed, and when the rate environment moves, both your monthly payment and your home's valuation get levered by it. Third, it trains a way of thinking: **any label that says "safe" should be cracked open to see how much hidden leverage sits inside.** Stock risk is visible; duration risk is invisible — and the invisible kind is usually more expensive.

## 3. 🧬 Biology — The Octopus's Brain Lives in Eight Arms

If Earth contains a genuinely "alien" intelligence, the octopus is the proof God left lying around.

An octopus has roughly 500 million neurons — about as many as a dog. The key is the distribution: about two-thirds of them live not in its walnut-sized central brain but in its eight arms, each of which carries its own ganglia — effectively its own "mini-brain." Movement is not dispatched arm-by-arm from a central command; arms act semi-autonomously, exploring and grasping on their own, and a severed arm will keep recoiling from threats. It tastes and smells the world through its suckers, and its skin carries photoreceptors that let it "see" its surroundings and recolor itself to match the background within milliseconds — even when blind, its camouflage stays precise.

What raises goosebumps is its lineage. The last common ancestor of octopus and human was a flatworm-like creature on the seafloor more than 600 million years ago. After that, human intelligence took one road (vertebrate, heavily centralized brain, social, linguistic, intergenerational), and the octopus took a completely unrelated one (cephalopod, distributed neurons, solitary, a one-to-two-year lifespan, no parent teaching its young). **This is intelligence arising on Earth a second, independent time** — a species sharing zero social structure, zero language, zero culture with you, yet it opens jars, escapes aquariums, carries coconut shells as armor, recognizes individual humans, and plays. It even edits its own RNA on a massive scale, so its proteins don't match its DNA — an entire layer of information processing layered atop genetics that we barely understand.

For someone building AI, this is a from-the-roots challenge. Your default template for "intelligence" is one central brain + a language + a socially-learned training set. The octopus, with half a billion neurons, falsifies that template's uniqueness: **intelligence can exist without language, without centralized control, and can emerge spontaneously in a species with no social learning at all.** Every centralizing assumption you bake into your "multi-agent systems" and "embodied agents" — every "you need language before you get reasoning" premise — collapses in front of an eight-armed animal. If you want to see how coordinated behavior emerges from decentralized control, stop reading papers and go watch an octopus.

## 4. 🌏 Macro — Dollar Hegemony's Destiny Is to Run a Deficit Forever

Here is a coincidence that reads like history: on August 15, 1971, Nixon announced the closing of the gold window, and Bretton Woods collapsed on the spot. Exactly 55 years later, to the day, you are reading this. And inside that collapse lies a knot nobody has untied, called the **Triffin dilemma**.

At Bretton Woods in 1944, the dollar was installed as the world's reserve currency, pegged to gold at $35 an ounce, with other currencies pegged to the dollar. In 1960, the Belgian-American economist Robert Triffin saw through the system's self-destruct logic: for the world to trade, hold reserves, and settle, it must continuously accumulate dollars — which means the U.S. must continuously ship dollars abroad, i.e., run **persistent trade deficits** (more dollars out than back). But the bigger the deficits pile up, the more dollars the world holds, and eventually people begin to doubt whether a dollar is still "as good as gold" — triggering a run. So the system is caught in a dilemma: the world needs dollars (requiring U.S. deficits), yet too many dollars destroy trust (requiring U.S. surpluses) — and you can't have both. In 1971 the doubt became reality, and Nixon's speech was the closing act of that knot.

But the most counterintuitive part: after the dollar was cut loose from gold, the Triffin dilemma didn't vanish — it just changed clothes. Today the U.S. still supplies global dollar liquidity through deficits, and in return collects what French finance minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing called the "exorbitant privilege" — the ability to borrow from the world in its own currency at rock-bottom rates. That is why U.S. Treasuries are the "risk-free rate" benchmark for global asset pricing, and why nearly everything you touch daily — your AI cloud bills, your funding, commodity quotes — is dollar-denominated by default.

For you, this backdrop matters because the "defaults" in your head — the risk-free rate, the dollar's permanence, Treasuries' safety — are all bets on the Triffin system continuing to spin. And today's de-dollarization debate (central banks buying gold at record pace, BRICS pushing local-currency settlement, the digital yuan) is really one question: how much longer can this half-century-old arrangement hold? The dollar still accounts for roughly 58% of global FX reserves — still overwhelmingly dominant — but it's the *slope* of that trend, not the level, that deserves your attention. **Every financial foundation you think is neutral actually stands on a historical, expiry-dated agreement.**

- 📖 Barry Eichengreen, *Exorbitant Privilege* — how the dollar rose, and why it won't fall easily
- 📄 Robert Triffin, *Gold and the Dollar Crisis* (1960) — the original statement of the knot
- 🔗 IMF COFER data — track the dollar's share of global reserves and watch which way it drifts each year

## 5. ⚖️ Law & Economics — Coase's Thought Experiment That Rewrote How the World Sees Property

In 1960, a British economist named Ronald Coase wrote a paper called "The Problem of Social Cost," overturned a generation's intuition in a few pages, and later collected the 1991 Nobel Prize for it.

The starting question is humble: externalities. A factory's smoke dirties a neighbor's laundry; a train's sparks set fire to crops by the tracks. Who should bear the cost? The standard answer for a century was Pigouvian: punish the offender, tax them, legislate a ban. Coase said: all wrong. He offered a symmetrical insight — **harm is reciprocal**: avoiding harm to B is itself a harm to A. Shut the factory and the neighbor's laundry is clean, but the factory owner loses his livelihood. The real question is not "who harms whom," but "who has the right to do what, and who pays for it."

From this he derived the theorem that now bears his name: **if transaction costs are zero and property rights are clearly defined, the parties will bargain to the same efficient outcome regardless of who initially holds the right.** If the factory has the right to pollute, the neighbor pays it to install a filter; if the neighbor has the right to clean air, the factory pays to buy the right. Who pays changes (that determines the distribution of wealth), but "how much pollution happens" — the efficiency result — stays the same. Who owns the right affects only who gets richer, not how the problem is solved.

Of course, in reality transaction costs are never zero — bargaining is expensive, parties are many, information is asymmetric. So the job Coase reassigned to the law is this: **assign the right to whoever minimizes transaction costs, so the law mimics the outcome the market would have reached.** That one idea rebuilt half the institutions around you: radio spectrum went from bureaucratic allocation to auctions (America's first major spectrum auction in 1994 was the direct descendant of Coase's 1959 paper "The Federal Communications Commission"); emissions were turned into tradeable permits, birthing carbon markets — and when the U.S. used this logic to fight acid rain in 1990, the cost came in at a fraction of predictions.

The edge this gives a technologist: when you hit a dispute, your instinct is to reach for a *rule* — ban it, tax it, legislate a blanket ban. Coase makes you pause and ask three questions first: is the property right here even clearly defined? How high are the costs of striking a deal? And could you **define a right and let the parties trade**, more cheaply than any centralized rule? From scraping-copyright fights, to open-source licenses, to "who owns AI training data," these are all the same Coasean question — and most "tragedies of the commons" have nothing to do with moral failure, and everything to do with a property right that was never defined.

- 📖 Ronald Coase, *The Firm, the Market, and the Law* — contains the full text of "The Problem of Social Cost," short and hard
- 📄 Coase (1959), "The Federal Communications Commission" — the intellectual origin of spectrum auctions
