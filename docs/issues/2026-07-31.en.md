## Two thousand years ago, a crippled slave split life into two halves — and the half you can't control is far larger than you think

Around 55 CE, a Phrygian slave named Epictetus began his philosophical inquiry in the Roman Empire. His leg was deliberately broken by his master — according to the account, he remained calm throughout, saying only: "You will break it." He later gained his freedom and opened a philosophy school in Nicopolis, northwestern Greece, where he taught until his death around 135 CE. He wrote nothing. All his ideas were recorded by his student Arrian, who transcribed his classroom dialogues into two slim volumes: the *Enchiridion* (Handbook) and the *Discourses*.

The *Enchiridion* opens with one of the most violent first sentences in Western philosophy — no preamble, no rhetorical warm-up, just a hand pressing your neck downward:

**"Some things are up to us, and some things are not up to us."**

Up to us: judgment, impulse, desire, aversion — in short, everything that belongs to our own mental activity. Not up to us: body, property, reputation, career — in sum, everything that does not belong to our own mental activity.

This is not feel-good advice. This is not "just relax and don't worry." Epictetus is following a logic that makes you uncomfortable: **if you tie your well-being to something you cannot control, you are choosing misery by design.** The stock goes up — you're happy. It goes down — you're miserable. You handed the remote control to the market and then wondered why your emotions don't obey you. You wrote something, obsess over its readership and reception — you gave the keys to your emotional state to every reader you'll never meet. Epictetus's question is: who is in charge here?

The most brutal application came from Vietnam. U.S. Admiral James Stockdale was shot down in 1965 and spent seven and a half years as a POW in Hanoi — four of them in solitary confinement, repeatedly tortured. He later wrote in his memoir that the single most important cognitive tool for surviving prison was Epictetus's dichotomy. What surprised him most was who *didn't* survive: the optimists. The ones who kept saying "We'll be out by Christmas" — when Christmas came and they weren't out, they broke. Then "We'll be out by Easter" — and broke again. Round after round, hope curdling into despair, until the person shattered. The Stoic prisoners — the ones who didn't predict when they'd be released, who focused only on what they could do each day: maintain dignity, help fellow prisoners, refuse to betray anything to interrogators — they were the ones who walked out alive.

"You always have a choice" is a phrase people throw around, but most of the time they mean you can pick Company A or Company B, this approach or that one. Epictetus meant something far more radical: **when every external option has been stripped away — when you cannot choose where to go, what to do, who to be with — you can still choose how you judge what is happening.** This is the last territory that cannot be taken. If you surrender this too, you are no longer a free agent. You are an automatic response mechanism.

For someone working in the AI era, this dichotomy has a cold modern variant. The industry's technical direction does not fully depend on you. Capital market preferences do not fully depend on you. Your company's board decisions do not fully depend on you. What your competitors are doing does not fully depend on you. The trajectory of AI capabilities does not fully depend on you. Add all of these up and the domain you actually control looks like a tiny island. But the quality of your work on that island — how you choose problems, how you train your judgment, how you treat colleagues, where you invest your attention every day — depends entirely on you, and no one and nothing can take that away. And the irony: in the long run, the people who get the best external returns are precisely the ones who invest the most in the controllable domain. Not because hard work attracts luck — but because they spent their energy on the one thing luck can't wipe out.

Getting started: Epictetus's *Enchiridion* is about forty pages and can be read on a subway ride — the Robin Hard translation from Oxford World's Classics is excellent, with helpful notes. For applying Stoicism to modern life, William B. Irvine's *A Guide to the Good Life: The Ancient Art of Stoic Joy* is the best entry point — academically rigorous but never dry, and it refines the dichotomy into a practical "trichotomy of control" (total control / partial control / no control). Stockdale's memoir *Thoughts of a Philosophical Fighter Pilot* is also worth reading — more persuasive than any academic study of Stoicism, because he's not explaining the theory, he's explaining how he survived by it.

## The regulator's fridge contains salmon sent by the regulated — this isn't corruption, this is design

In 1971, University of Chicago economist George Stigler published a paper. It later won him the 1982 Nobel Prize in Economics, but its deeper impact was explaining a chilling institutional phenomenon: **regulatory capture.**

Stigler's core argument is plain and subversive: regulation is not a story of an independent public-interest agent battling greedy industries. Regulation itself is a **commodity**, supplied by political systems and purchased by industries. An industry can use political lobbying, information advantages, and personnel exchange to "buy" favorable regulation — such as erecting entry barriers to block potential competitors, or setting compliance standards just high enough that small firms can't afford them. Stigler's own words: **"As a rule, regulation is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit."**

The most unsettling thing about capture isn't corruption — at least not the envelope-under-the-table kind. It's more subtle, more respectable, more **legal**. Its core mechanism is the revolving door: regulatory agencies need people who understand the industry, so they hire from the industry. These people serve a few years as regulators, then return to the industry at high salaries — and every instance of "gentle enforcement" and "flexible interpretation" during their regulatory tenure was quietly building goodwill for the next career move. Nobody needs to say "go easy on us and we'll give you a job." Structural incentives do all the work themselves.

The case list is numbing. Before the 2008 financial crisis, the SEC had acquired new authority in 2004 to regulate investment banks uniformly — and chose not to exercise it. The Office of Thrift Supervision (OTS), the agency tasked with overseeing AIG, had fewer staff than a single AIG trading desk. Before the Deepwater Horizon explosion, officials at the Minerals Management Service (MMS) were golfing with oil company executives, in what an internal investigation called a "pervasive culture of coziness." None of these are movie scenes where people exchange briefcases in parking lots. **These are conference rooms filled with people who genuinely believe "industry self-regulation is the most efficient form of oversight" — because their brains have been colonized by the industry's worldview.**

Regulatory capture theory has direct bearing on today's AI governance debates. When you see proposals for "AI safety should be achieved through self-regulation by leading labs," the capture framework tells you: this is not a safety strategy, this is an administrative vacuum. Letting a profit-seeking entity regulate itself is neither regulation nor self-discipline — it's an invitation for capture to complete itself at maximum speed. When a government agency recruits AI technical advisors, those advisors will almost inevitably come from the top companies in the industry they'll regulate — and their compensation upon returning to industry depends on how much capital they accumulated while in government. This is not conspiracy theory. It's the incentive structure Stigler proved in 1971.

A deeper structural problem: **the captured regulator often genuinely believes they're doing the right thing.** When an FAA official believes "what's good for Boeing is good for aviation safety," that's not hypocrisy — that's cognitive capture. When everyone around you, every conference you attend, every industry report you read tells you the same narrative, resisting it requires more than moral courage. It requires **alternative information sources** — something a captured person has almost no way to acquire.

Getting started: Stigler's 1971 original, *The Theory of Economic Regulation*, is more readable than its journal placement suggests — it has that crisp old Chicago School elegance. For a contemporary follow-up, Brink Lindsey and Steven Teles's *The Captured Economy: How the Powerful Enrich Themselves, Slow Down Growth, and Increase Inequality* applies capture theory to occupational licensing, land use, finance, and intellectual property. For AI governance specifically, Stanford HAI's piece *The Revolving Door in AI Policy* provides concrete agency-to-industry personnel flow data — a solid empirical entry point.

## Your brain can only sustain 150 meaningful relationships. Beyond that, you must invent bureaucracy — and it's bureaucracy you hate most

In 1992, Oxford anthropologist Robin Dunbar published a paper with a title too plain to foreshadow the impact it would have: *Neocortex size as a constraint on group size in primates*. His method was elegant: take the neocortex-to-whole-brain volume ratio across different primate species, run a regression against their average social group size. The result was a near-perfect fit — and plugging in the human neocortex data gave **147.8, or roughly 150.**

This is what people now call Dunbar's number: **the cognitive limit on the number of stable social relationships a human can maintain.** "Stable" here has a clear operational definition: you know who each person is, you know how they relate to others in the group, and you can interact smoothly without needing to be reintroduced. This is not your LinkedIn contact count. This is the number of people you maintain bidirectional awareness with — you remember what you last talked about, you could call them at 2 AM if necessary.

The evidence spans an absurd range. Neolithic village sites average about 150 residents. Roman army centuries, despite the name, held 100–130 soldiers — right in the band. Hutterite communities have an explicit rule: when a settlement exceeds 150, it splits, because beyond that number, "peer pressure" as a governance mechanism stops working. Modern surveys find that the average Brit sends Christmas cards to 153.5 people — within the margin of a dinner party. Facebook's internal research found that although users average hundreds of "friends," the number of people with whom they maintain bidirectional interaction is — around 150.

Dunbar's number isn't a behavioral norm. It's a neurological constraint. More precisely, it's the centerpiece of the **Social Brain Hypothesis**: primates evolved larger brains not to solve tool use, foraging strategies, or predator evasion — but to handle **social complexity**. In a troop of 50 baboons, you need to track every individual's rank, who is allied with whom, who has old scores to settle, whose infant you can play with and whose you can't — this is a combinatorial-explosion computational problem, and the large neocortex evolved to compute it.

Gore-Tex — the waterproof fabric company — is one of the few organizations that takes Dunbar's number seriously as an operating principle. Their factories are hard-capped at 150 people. When a unit exceeds that, it splits into two fully independent factories. No hierarchy, no middle managers, no formal job descriptions. In a 150-person factory, everyone knows everyone, and you don't need an org chart — social cognition handles coordination spontaneously. Gore-Tex's turnover rate consistently runs under one-third of the industry average.

**Above 150, abstract rules must replace personal relationships as the coordination mechanism.** You need job descriptions — because you can't possibly know everyone's specific capabilities. You need performance reviews — because trust can no longer substitute for oversight. You need hierarchy — because information can no longer flow through informal channels. Dunbar himself noted in a 1998 follow-up that modern human societies reach millions and billions in scale because we invented three things to breach the 150 barrier: language (letting others maintain relationship information for you), institutions (abstract rules replacing interpersonal trust), and religion/ideology (shared behavioral codes among strangers). But the core tension never resolved: **humans are designed to operate in groups of 150, yet are required to coordinate with thousands in modern organizations.**

The implication for founders is direct and brutal. Before 150 people, your organization is a tribe. After 150, your organization is a system. Before 150, "we don't need management" is true — everyone knows each other, coordination is spontaneous. After 150, that statement becomes "we are pretending we don't need management, until the day we can no longer pretend." Most companies' agony between 50 and 300 people isn't about business — it's about switching from a spontaneously-coordinating tribe to an artificially-regulated institution, while the entire organization is nostalgic for the tribal era. The nostalgia is justified — but it cannot reverse neurobiology.

Getting started: Dunbar's *How Many Friends Does One Person Need?* balances academic rigor with readability. The original paper *Neocortex size as a constraint on group size in primates* (1992, *Journal of Human Evolution*) is short and worth reading directly if you're interested in the evolutionary model. A strongly recommended Twitter thread from @emollick (Ethan Mollick) in 2023, "The 150 Rule," covers Dunbar's number applied to organizational management more clearly than most books in twenty tweets. And an adjacent-but-relevant read: Sebastian Junger's *Tribe*, on the deep human need for small-scale community and how modern society systematically deprives us of it.

## Bacteria never signed the rule that says genes only flow from parent to child — they run an open gene flea market, and your body has been shopping there for millions of years

The genetics we learned in high school is restrained, proper, unidirectional: DNA passes from parent to offspring, vertically downward. Mutations occur occasionally, natural selection filters across generations. A clean timeline. One species, one gene pool. A branching tree.

**Bacteria did not sign this contract.**

Gene transfer among bacteria happens through three main routes: transformation — absorbing free-floating DNA fragments released into the environment by dead bacteria; conjugation — two bacteria build a temporary cytoplasmic bridge and transfer a circular DNA molecule called a plasmid from one to the other; transduction — a virus, acting as a gene courier, accidentally packages a fragment of bacterial DNA during infection and injects it into the next bacterial host.

Taken together, these three mechanisms create a gene-circulation model unique in the biological world: **horizontal gene transfer (HGT).** No kinship required. No shared species. Not even the requirement that both parties be alive at the same time. A soil bacterium can transfer an antibiotic-resistance gene to a gut pathogen it has never met, because somewhere in between, a floating strand of DNA acted as an envelope-less letter.

The most direct consequence of HGT is the exponential spread of antibiotic resistance. The traditional imagination says resistance follows standard evolution: a bacterium randomly mutates resistance → it survives antibiotic pressure → its offspring inherit the trait. But HGT turns this from "generational inheritance" into "LAN sharing." One bacterium acquires a resistance gene and can transfer it via plasmid conjugation to hundreds of different species across the entire local microbiome in a matter of hours. You don't even need antibiotics to observe resistance genes flowing through wild ecosystems — a 2011 *Nature* metagenomic study of 30,000-year-old permafrost, utterly untouched by human antibiotics, detected β-lactamase resistance genes nearly identical to contemporary clinical ones. These genes predated human antibiotic use by roughly 29,940 years. Resistance isn't something we created — it's an ancient, ecosystem-level gene circulation network; antibiotics merely gave it a strong directional pressure.

But the more disruptive insight is evolutionary. Horizontal gene transfer means **the tree of life is not a tree.** At the microbial level — and even higher, as the evolutionary records of early eukaryotes are littered with HGT evidence — the picture of evolution looks more like a web, with horizontal gene flow nearly as important as vertical inheritance. Some scientists estimate that 20% to 80% of prokaryotic genomes may be horizontally acquired, depending on species and environment. This means the very concept of "species" needs redefinition in the microbial world — because the genetic difference between two bacteria may not come from divergent ancestral lineages, but from which genes they each recently "downloaded."

And here's the most unsettling twist: **mammals — including humans — are beneficiaries of horizontal gene transfer.** Roughly 8% of the human genome consists of endogenous retroviruses (ERVs). These are ancient viral infections that, after infecting a host cell, reverse-transcribed their RNA into DNA and integrated into the host's germline genome — then got passed down through generations, permanently locked into the species' genetic code. The most famous example is **syncytin** — a gene originally from a viral envelope protein, now repurposed by mammals for embryo implantation, enabling placental cells to fuse and form the maternal-fetal interface. Without this viral gene, the placenta cannot form. In other words: **a virus taught mammals how to be mothers.**

Horizontal gene transfer turns the narrative of evolution from a solemn family tree into an improvisational jam session. Bacteria swapped countless lines of "code" across countless nights, some of which larger organisms forked into their own repositories and never returned. Nearly one-tenth of your DNA comes from this unauthorized, continent-spanning, geological-era pull request. This is not a poetic metaphor. It is precise biochemical fact.

Getting started: Ed Yong's *I Contain Multitudes* is the pinnacle of microbiome writing — horizontal gene transfer threads through every chapter, not as a theme but as part of the fabric. Carl Zimmer's *A Planet of Viruses* focuses on the viral perspective and digs deep into the ERV and syncytin story. For a review paper, Ochman, Lawrence, and Groisman's 1999 *Nature* piece *Lateral gene transfer and the nature of bacterial innovation* remains, more than two decades later, the best entry point into the field. A shorter but equally powerful read is the Wikipedia entry on "Horizontal gene transfer" — remarkably complete, with diagrams for each mechanism.

## A Bell Labs physicist discovered that the optimal bet size and the optimal information encoding rate are the same mathematical problem — and it changed the careers of Warren Buffett and Edward Thorp

In 1956, Bell Labs scientist John Larry Kelly Jr. — Texan, physicist, fond of cowboy hats — published a paper in the lab's internal journal. Its title was surgical: *A New Interpretation of Information Rate*. It proposed a mathematical formula now known as the **Kelly Criterion**:

> f\* = (bp − q) / b

Where f\* is your optimal bet fraction, b is the odds (net payout per unit wagered), p is your true win probability, and q = 1 − p.

The core question is disarmingly bold: **if you know the odds are mispriced — you know your win probability is higher than the bookmaker thinks — how much should you bet?**

Intuition says: the bigger the edge, the more you bet. Kelly says: not quite. The formula sizes your bet in proportion to your edge but in inverse proportion to volatility. If your edge is small, bet small. If your edge is large, bet large — but never everything. **Never all-in.** Even with a 99% win probability, the Kelly formula won't let you bet 100% — because if that 1% failure probability hits, your bankroll goes to zero and the game ends. In geometric-mean-return terms, bankruptcy is an irreversible terminal state. Zero times any return rate is zero.

A counterintuitive corollary: **betting too much is worse than betting too little.** If you bet more than twice the Kelly fraction (f > 2f\*), your expected growth rate turns negative. Even if every individual bet has positive expected value, your wealth heads steadily toward zero. This sounds paradoxical, but it's the difference between arithmetic and geometric mean. A stock goes up 50% one day, down 40% the next — arithmetic average return is +5%, but your asset is at 0.9x. The larger the bet, the greater the volatility, the heavier the geometric drag. The Kelly Criterion is the precise controller of this drag.

The deeper shock of Kelly's paper isn't its casino application — though it did produce the MIT blackjack team, Edward Thorp, and an entire generation of quantitative hedge fund founders — but its theoretical origin. Kelly wasn't a gambler; he was Claude Shannon's colleague at Bell Labs. Shannon had just founded information theory, proving that in a noisy communication channel, there exists a maximum reliable transmission rate — the channel capacity. Kelly realized: **the optimal encoding rate for information and the optimal bet size for gambling are the same mathematical problem.** Both involve a time series where the goal isn't maximizing any single step's return but maximizing long-term growth rate. Shannon's channel capacity formula and Kelly's betting formula are mathematically isomorphic. This means: **gambling is information processing.** Your edge is your information advantage, your bet size is your conviction strength, your bankroll growth is the compounding of your cognition in the real world.

The investment implications were pushed to the extreme by Edward Thorp — mathematician, inventor of the blackjack card-counting system, and later founder of Princeton/Newport Partners, one of the earliest and most successful quantitative hedge funds. Throughout his career, Thorp treated the Kelly Criterion as his betting bible. In his autobiography *A Man for All Markets*, he states that all else equal, **the Kelly Criterion is the only strategy that maximizes long-term growth while guaranteeing never going broke.** Thorp's fund delivered 19.1% annualized returns over twenty years, with no single year losing more than 1%. That's not luck — that's Kelly.

For someone in AI and tech, the Kelly Criterion has an under-appreciated application: **your career is your largest position.** If your entire human capital is highly correlated with the tech sector, your equity is in the same sector, and your personal investment portfolio is heavily weighted toward tech stocks — you are effectively running three to four times leverage on the same signal, far exceeding any reasonable Kelly fraction. A single industry-level drawdown simultaneously hits your income, your equity net worth, and your personal account — all in the same direction. The first step in diversification isn't picking what stocks to buy — it's seeing what you've already bet on.

Getting started: William Poundstone's *Fortune's Formula: The Untold Story of the Scientific Betting System That Beat the Casinos and Wall Street* is a biographical and scientific tour de force — weaving together Shannon, Kelly, Thorp, and the mafia into a gripping narrative, with the math explained superbly. Thorp's *A Man for All Markets* tells the Kelly story from the operator's perspective and is even richer. If you just want the formula and its derivation, J. L. Kelly Jr.'s original 1956 paper *A New Interpretation of Information Rate* is only fourteen pages and available as a free PDF online — reading how a physicist reinvented gambling through information theory is a rare experience.
